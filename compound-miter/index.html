Crown molding angles, explained

Crown molding does not sit flat against the wall. It is sprung across the corner between wall and ceiling, so a cut that looks like a simple miter from the front is actually two angles at once: the blade has to swing and tilt. That pair — miter and bevel — is what this page gives you for any corner.

Why 45 degrees fails on most corners

45 is only correct on a corner that is exactly 90 degrees. Very few are: framing is rarely dead square, drywall corner bead rounds the inside corner, and plaster walls can be several degrees out. A corner measured at 92 degrees on 38 spring crown needs miter 30.7 and bevel 33.2, not 31.6 and 33.9 — enough to leave a gap you can see. Measure, do not assume.

Miter versus bevel: which one fixes which gap

They fail differently, and knowing which is off saves a lot of scrap. Rocking assembly or a joint that will not close along its length means the bevel is wrong. The pieces meet at the face but a gap opens along the seam. A gap on the outside face with the pieces sitting flat means the miter is wrong. Change one at a time; if you move both you learn nothing about which was off.

Where the long point goes

Every crown cut has a long point and a short point because the profile is cut at an angle. On an inside corner the long point is the bottom edge, the one that touches the wall. On an outside corner the long point is the top edge, against the ceiling. Cut to the long point, not the short one, or the piece comes up short at the corner.

Nested versus flat: two valid methods, two different numbers

Nested means the molding sits upside down in the saw, resting on its back feet the way it will hang on the wall. That needs only a miter — 180 minus the corner, divided by two — and no bevel at all, because the molding is already at its spring angle. It is faster and it is what many pros do. But it only works when the back is flat and the feet register square, and tall crown may not clear the blade. Flat on the table works on any profile, which is why the numbers here are for flat-down. Never mix the two sets of numbers: nested miter for a square corner is 45, flat-down miter is 35.3 for 45 spring.

Check the saw before you trust the scale

Printed miter and bevel scales on consumer saws are commonly a degree or more out, and a degree of bevel error spread across sixteen cut faces on an octagon is visible. Use a digital angle gauge against the blade, and cut test pieces from the same stock thickness as the finished work.
