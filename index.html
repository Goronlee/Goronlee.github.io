What is a board foot?

A board foot is a volume unit for lumber: 144 cubic inches — a board 12 in long, 12 in wide, and 1 in thick. Hardwood in North America is billed by the board foot, but it is sold as whole boards: the bill says board feet, the rack says boards.

How do you calculate board feet?

For a board: thickness (in) × width (in) × length (ft) ÷ 12. Example: 2 × 6 × 8 ÷ 12 = 8 bf. For a project part, the thickness that matters is the rough quarter thickness you buy, not the finished dimension: a 3/4″ finished panel is cut from 4/4 (1″) stock, and a 1-1/2″ finished leg needs 8/4 (2″) stock — 4/4 planed is only 13/16″ thick and can never yield a 1-1/2″ part.

Why quarter thickness ≠ finished thickness?

Rough-sawn hardwood comes in quarters: 4/4 = 1″, 5/4 = 1.25″, 6/4 = 1.5″, 8/4 = 2″. Surfacing removes 3/16″ (up to 6/4) or 1/4″ (8/4 and thicker): 4/4 S2S measures about 13/16″ but is still billed as 1″. Planning from finished thickness — the only number on your cut list — is exactly what this calculator does for you.

Why is a shopping list more than total board feet?

Three structural gaps sit between "my parts total X bf" and "what to buy": (1) parts longer than a board can never come out of it; (2) ripping narrow parts from a wide board wastes 10–35% depending on the width remainder — 6″ and 8″ boards are classic trap widths for 1.5″ parts; (3) a 32″ part on a 96″ board yields only 2 pieces, not 3, once the saw kerf is added. This tool models all three, per part, and outputs boards.
